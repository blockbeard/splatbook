Dragons are a mystery. Dragons make dungeons full of magical traps. No one knows why. Dragons do not speak the common language, and attempts to communicate with them have driven sorcerers insane. They may not be evil, but they are so far away from humanity that you might as well call them such.

Mesi, the ancient philosopher, theorized that dragons domesticate and cultivate humans in the same way humans cultivate cattle, and no modern discussion of dragons fails to bring up (and usually disagree with) this curious point. The fact is, nobody knows much about dragons. (Dragons steal princesses, too. No one is sure why, either.)

# The GM’s Manifesto

> [!lede]
> A game is all about decisions. That is essentially what every game is, whether it's a traditional game like poker, or a board game, or an RPG.

A role-playing game’s _play_ happens via discussion. The GM describes things, the players ask questions, the GM elaborates, the players describe things, the GM asks questions, the players elaborate, and the process repeats. It’s a back-and-forth—asking questions and answering them. During this exchange, the GM and the players make decisions. This is what’s fun about RPGs.

Therefore, your job is to create interesting decisions for the players. Some advice to this end:

- Create environments that incite interesting decisions

- Create characters and factions with interesting motivations

- Describe the environment fully, engaging sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch

- Act in good faith by speaking generously

- Referee player actions fairly

### Create meaningful exploration

Decisions in an RPG should be interesting, not arbitrary. It is essential that, during the Crawl, you provide meaningful choices so that exploration is both fun and engaging.

An empty hallway with two branching paths doesn’t give the players enough information to make an informed or interesting choice. A coin flip could determine the “best” way to go. However, you could put Cant graffiti on the walls of the right path that reads: “Undead ahead! Do not enter! Turn back! All is lost!” If the players listen at the left path, they could hear the distinctively peacock-esque cry of the cockatrice. Now the players have information to make a decision: would they rather face a cockatrice or the undead? What are they most prepared for? Could they lure the undead towards the cockatrice?

Broadcast dangers. Hide treasures behind clues that make players feel clever. Create meaningful choices so that your players can make informed decisions.

### Create engaging personas

The Underworld and the City should both be full of memorable characters with their own interesting motivations. Each of the GM’s characters has a personality defined by their likes, dislikes, wants, and needs.

In the same way that dangers should be broadcast, so should your characters’ personalities. By clearly showing what a character’s likes and dislikes are, players will be given opportunities to role-play meaningfully.

The Underworld is deadly enough that not every single denizen needs to be hostile. The Disposition rules ([[06 - Chapter 6 - The Crawl Phase#Social Encounters & Disposition|Social Encounters & Disposition]]) make sure that players have a fair shake when encountering new characters. Give players opportunities to gather information, make unlikely allies, and take part in factional intrigue.

### Engage the senses

Meaningful exploration only works when the players have a good sense of what is going on. Players need you to tell them as much information as possible about the environment they are acting within. Paint pictures with your descriptions, actually sketch out rooms when mental pictures are difficult to conjure, and be vivid about all five senses. Don’t leave out any salient details about things that players can see, hear, feel, or smell.

### Speak generously

Don’t fall into the trap of thinking that half-secrets and hints are interesting. Information-sharing games are only fun when information is actually shared. Maybe the information comes with a price (“You’re not sure if the dragon is truly sleeping or only faking it. You’ll have to move closer to get a better look at him…”), but when it comes time to pay the piper, give the players as much information as you can. “Gotcha” moments are not fun.

This is particularly true when players bid lore. If you accept a lore bid, you have a social obligation to speak generously to the player and give as much information as is appropriate.

### Be a fair arbiter
> [!sidebar] Arbitrating Rules Questions
>
> What should be done if there is a rules dispute during gameplay? One of the GM's duties is to settle these questions and decide how the rule should be played. If you're still learning the game, you might want to look them up every time until the flow of narrative and mechanics feels natural. If you have the rules down pretty well but encounter a speedbump, we offer this suggestion: decide in favor of the players and look up the rule later. Don't slow down a fun combat with page turning. Just make a temporary rule that is beneficial for the players and ask your best rules lawyer at the table to look it up after the combat is over.

Let player decisions carry weight. If the players come up with ideas that should work, you don’t even need to touch the cards. Their plan can simply work. What makes sense comes before the rules.

At the same time, don’t pull punches. Victories are only valuable if defeat is a real possibility. You are neither the players’ antagonist nor their friend. One of your roles is to be a neutral interpreter of the _physics_ of the imaginary world.

There are random elements. Interpret them judiciously. As much as possible, make the mechanics visible. Say what you’re doing, what the players are testing fate for, and what the results will be.

As the GM, you can give difficult situations to the players with _no idea how to solve them yourself._ The players will surprise you with their ingenuity and **problem-solving skills. When they come up with something surprising and cool** that _should_ work, let it work.

# Running the Crawl Phase

> [!lede]
> The standard flow of the Crawl is as follows:

The players declare where they intend to go. If the guild begins this phase by leaving the City, they declare which entrance to the **Underworld** they will use. If the guild is in the Underworld, they declare the direction they move (e.g., “We’ll go towards room 12 to the east.”)

- The GM draws a card from the major arcana for the [[06 - Chapter 6 - The Crawl Phase#Meatgrinder|Meatgrinder]].

- The GM asks, “What light do you have?

Based on what the players can see, the GM describes the room’s contents in addition to the Meatgrinder results.

The GM asks, “What do you do?”

- The players ask the GM questions about the environment.

- The players describe their actions.

- To help adjudicate, the GM asks, “What do you have in your hands?”

The GM arbitrates the results of the players’ actions. The GM calls for tests of fate when the situation is tense and failure is interesting. This entire process repeats.

## Creating your Underworld
> [!sidebar] Homebrewing
>
> Running a game is sort of like cooking. The first few times you do it, you adhere judiciously to a recipe. However, the more you cook, the more that repetition, experimentation, improvisation, and restriction reveals the methodology and chemistry that underlies the recipes.
>
> This game is a complete package but it is just a recipe. It has an underlying chemistry that produces a particular taste. The more you play it, the more you’ll understand how the parts and pieces work together. Once you do, you’ll understand how to change parts of it to better suit your tastes. The game is better when the book is not the authority; rather, authority is given to the GM and the general will of the table.
>
> If this book mentions something in passing like, say, lycanthropy, we haven’t felt the need to give you paragraphs and paragraphs of crunch on the subject. RPGs have been going on for a few decades now. There’s information out there. Find some time between sessions to create some lycanthropy rules that you like. Make the lore work for the game world that you’ve made. Get player buy-in about the idea and get everybody excited about it.

You own the act of mapping the dungeon, inventing the stats for monsters, designing traps to be overcome, and hiding rewards. _His Majesty the Worm_ front-loads this work. Before you start running a game, you’ll want to sit down and sketch out the entire Underworld megadungeon that you’ll be using. Appendix E deals with generating your Underworld megadungeon.

Doing this work over the course of one or two Sunday afternoons frees you from doing weekly game session planning. You’ve done the hard work already. The City Phase will be your trigger to keep your dungeons fresh: this is when you think about how the players have changed the situation in the Underworld, update your notes accordingly, and create new Meatgrinder entries to replace the ones the players have already seen.

### Creating a soundscape

You can provide a sense of consistency and theme for the different dungeons of your Underworld by building a bespoke playlist. Associate each level with a specific song. When the players enter a dungeon, play its theme song on a loop. (Choosing a music player with a looping option is important. Spotify works well.)

You can also have theme music for the beginning and ending of a session, for Challenges, and for recurring GM characters.

Video game music works well for this purpose. It’s designed to fade into the background and create an atmosphere. Be aware that your players may indelibly associate the music with that particular video game, taking them out of the _His Majesty the Worm_ experience. Choose your favorite music from obscure games or games you know your table hasn’t played overmuch.

_Your mileage may vary._ Some players find any music—even music without lyrics—to be very distracting. Ask your table what they prefer.

## Creating interesting characters

> [!lede]
> It’s your job to portray characters with personalities that are evocative, strange, conflicting, and clearly broadcast. This is true for all creatures that you control, no matter how monstrous!

Each character you introduce should have a personality defined by their dislikes and **likes, wants** and **needs**.

- **Dislikes** are gates to the character’s heart.

- **Likes** are the key.

- **Wants** are aspirational character desires.

- **Needs** are foundational character desires.

Understanding a character’s personality allows you to arbitrate the guild’s attempts to bargain with them fairly. Reference the Dispositions see [[06 - Chapter 6 - The Crawl Phase#Social Encounters & Disposition|Social Encounters & Disposition]] for role-playing guidance.

### Dislikes

Dislikes trigger negative Dispositions like distaste or anger.

- These triggers can be behaviors (“lying”) or traits (“all elves”).

- GM characters won’t bargain with adventurers that they dislike.

### Likes

Likes trigger positive Dispositions like trust or joy.

- These triggers can be behaviors (“sharing alcohol”) or traits (“good at music”).

- GM characters will bargain fairly with adventurers that they like.

### Wants

Wants fulfill the character’s wishes.

- Wants are discrete. They can be items (“enough money for a dowry”) or events (“marry the widow Prescott”).

- If a GM character is offered something they want as a bargaining chip, tests of fate to manipulate them are made with favor.

### Needs

Needs represent the status quo.

- Needs are abstract and essential (“for Baron Afanas to stay in power so the peasants continue to pay their taxes to me”).

- If a GM character’s needs are threatened as a bargaining chip, tests of Wands to manipulate them are made with favor but their Disposition will be a hostile one, such as anger or distaste. They won’t bargain again.

Contrasting or conflicting likes, dislikes, wants, and needs ensures that the players have multiple ways to interact with GM characters.

For instance, some mermaids might **like gossip** and **want to eat a man’s** eyes, **the tips of his fingers, and his privates**. A guild of men encountering mermaids are likely targets to be devoured. However, if the guild feed the mermaids juicy gossip instead, they might avoid a combat encounter.

## The blessing of chaos

Sometimes Meatgrinder events target random adventurers. Sometimes you can imagine a few equally reasonable possibilities and want some help making a fair decision. You can use the cards as a random oracle to help you make choices. Expose this randomness to your table. It will make you seem more “fair.” You can alleviate some of the harshest feelings of injustice by leaving an adventurer’s fate up to the cards.

Here are some tips for handling random events during the flow of gameplay.

### Events targeting an adventurer

Sometimes, a Meatgrinder event targets _one particular adventurer._ This should make sense in context: the first person in the marching order might blunder into the trap, whereas the last one in marching order doesn’t notice that their potion bottle is broken and leaking.

Sometimes, Meatgrinder events or an enemy’s attack targets a _random adventurer._ In this event, consult the top card of the minor arcana discard pile. The random effect will target the player whose path matches that suit. If you have multiple people with that path at the table, use the card’s value to choose between them in a quick way (e.g., odd is the first player to your left, even is the first player to your right).

### Events targeting equipment

You’ll notice that the adventurer sheet has four belt slots and twenty-one pack slots. These numbers were intentionally chosen.

If you need to target a player’s belt slot with a Meatgrinder event, glance at the minor arcana’s discard pile and note the top card’s suit for a random number 1–4.

## What are you holding?

There is one question that helps inform your adjudication of all other player actions: “What do you have in your hands right now?” **This is the most important question in your GM tool kit**. Anything an adventurer attempts might be hampered or helped by the answer.

### Example the first

**Player:** Can I climb the wall here and look over it?

**GM:** Well, the wall is made of crumbling brick, so sure, it would be easy. What are you carrying?

**Player:** I'll leave my sword and shield on the ground.

**GM:** OK. So, you prop your sword and shield against the wall and start climbing the wall?

### Example the second

**Player:** OK, I want to try and pick the lock on the weird door with the face.

**GM:** Who has the light source? (A variation on “What's in your hands?”)

Player 2: I do. I'm standing guard by the first room to make sure nobody ambushes us.

**GM:** If you're trying to pick the lock but your light source is at the cavern's opening, you basically can't see what you're doing. That'll be disfavor for sure.

**Player 3: I'll** pull out my torch and light it off of Player 2's and stand near the locked door to provide light.

**GM:** OK, that works. Test fate with Pentacles.

The fiction of what an adventurer is holding gives you immediate context for the rest of the scene.

## How much treasure?

You might ask “How much treasure should I give the players?” This is not the right way to think about it. Riches and life are both cheap, and can be quickly lost. Remember that the luxurious [[09 - Chapter 9 - The City Phase#^upkeep|upkeep]] in the City is 100 gold. Getting this can feel like a major reward. If players land a major haul, having a few City Phases where they live in the lap of luxury will reinforce the feeling that they had a major accomplishment. After that money is gone, they will return to more humble living.

## What can I see?

When a player asks what their adventurer can see, describe everything in the room that they could possibly see or notice. This gives players enough context to actually interact with the room in a meaningful way.

This information is filtered by a few factors, of course. An adventurer might not be able to see in the dark, but they could still hear their environment. They won't be able to see hidden or obscured objects (like a secret door or a thief hiding behind a curtain), but are free to search in more thorough ways.

Essentially, there are three levels of information based on how thoroughly an adventurer looks around them. No tests are required and no cards need to be drawn; the only thing that is tested is a player’s thoughtfulness while asking questions.

1. **Glancing:** This covers everything that can be perceived in an area by someone who has good light.

2. **Scrutinizing:** Gated, until the players ask questions. “Is the gargoyle made of stone?” “What color is the book?” “What are the pages made out of?” “Are the hinges inside or out?”

3. **Investigatory:** Gated, until the adventurers interact with the environment. “I try to twist the moose head. Does it feel like it has any give?” “**I’ll** pull back the tapestry. Is there anything behind it?” “I tap on the walls. Do they sound hollow?”

Information should only rarely be held back from the players. Be charitable in the information you give: the more information a player has, the more interesting choices they can make. So, even in a room with a secret door, the adventurer might feel a slight draft in the room that reveals that something is amiss. Or if there was a thief behind a curtain, they might hear a muffled cough for a moment, but then nothing.

## What do I know?

When a player asks, “Would my character know about X?” make a quick call on how obscure the question is. If the question is something their adventurer might possibly know, speak generously about the subject. If the question is obscure, ask the player if they have a motif they could use to bid [[02 - Chapter 2 - The Adventurer#10. Lore|lore]].

There are essentially two levels of knowledge.

1. **Obvious knowledge:** This covers stuff everybody knows and sets the general expectations of a situation. Giving players a lot of information makes sure that both players and the GM are on the same page and lets players make intelligent choices.

2. **Obscure knowledge:** Some specialist subjects are only going to be known to certain people. Obscure knowledge might include the habits of supernatural creatures, the likes and dislikes of specific people within an organization, or the metaphysics of the planes of reality. This information should only be given to players who **bid lore**.

If a player bids lore and you accept the question, give as much information on the subject as you know—just keep talking and riffing until you run out of material.

# Running the Challenge Phase

The Challenge rules resolve questions of timing between hostile forces in a fair and fun way. Actions like “We all run past the imps and slam the door” and “I want to shoot an arrow at the lich before they complete the ritual” are best answered by using the timing of the Challenge Phase.

The Crawl Phase can transition to the Challenge Phase and back organically. When there’s tension and a hostile situation does not seem resolvable with a single test of fate, call for a Challenge Phase. When the opposition is defeated, the outcome of the Challenge is clear, or the players want to retreat, transition back into the Crawl.

## Creating interesting locations

When possible, **Challenges should take place somewhere cool**.

Cool encounter spaces provide **potential energy—they** give the players (and the GM!) stuff to interact with. Scatter one or two interactable pieces of scenery across each zone, including dynamic **terrain**, **sensory information**, and **usable objects**.

### Terrain

Create spaces that tell a story. Challenges often inhabit a liminal space, set between safety and danger, stable and unstable, solid and liquid, cool and hot, heights and depths. Exploit these contrasts.

### Sensory information

An adventurer’s understanding of their environment is based on the limits of their senses. By default, the Underworld is a dark and difficult place. Place further restrictions on the guild’s vision, hearing, and other senses to provide additional challenges.

### Objects

Empty rooms are boring. Each space should be scattered with interesting items. Set pieces can be picked up, hidden behind, or smashed through.

| Terrain | Sensory information | Objects |
|---|---|---|
| A bridge over a chasm | Smoke near the room’s ceiling, choking or blinding tall characters | Hanging tapestries |
| A 45° collapsed obelisk | Fungus that extrudes choking spores, lights, or sounds when bumped into | Braziers of coals |
| Chains hanging from the ceiling | Fog near the room’s floor, obscuring short characters | Silk dressing screens |
| Icy floors | A colossal ringing bell, deafening all nearby | Tables and chairs |
| A climbable idol | Blinding light emanating from a bright red ruby | Bookshelves |
| Hanging chandeliers | The area is half-filled with murky water | Taxidermied monsters |
| Quaking floors | Illusory objects and creatures | Fountains |
| An oil slick | Shrieking wind, preventing flight or missile weapons | Weapon racks |

## Creating interesting battles

Your job as the GM is to keep the Challenge flowing. You track the Initiative count, lead discussions about rules, and make rule calls when players attempt different types of actions. And, importantly, you control the antagonists during the Challenge.

Here are some lessons learned during playtesting to help you create tactically difficult and rewarding Challenges.

### Gang up on the players

Players have more cards in total than the GM. They will act more often. However, you have an advantage when it comes to the number of antagonists.

- With multiple enemies, each action you use can target the same number of adventurers. If five goblins use the Attack action, this will affect up to five different adventurers.

- Enemies can also focus fire, attacking the _same_ adventurer. Use the rules for mobs see [[07 - Chapter 7 - The Challenge Phase#2. Play Initiative for each significant enemy|Play Initiative for each significant enemy]] to good effect.

- The GM’s “side” can often absorb more Wounds than the players.

The gimmick is that players feel cool as they mow down hordes of zombies or bandits or whatever. At the same time, it’s a war of attrition—the GM can **put** pressure on the players as they face hordes of enemies.

### Make minor actions as often as possible

You have much more freedom than players when it comes to card choice because you don’t have to worry about card suits. As such, take minor actions as often as possible.

Smart players will try to keep the monsters “stun-locked” with Roughhouse and various effects. Smart GMs will use Recover, which doesn’t have a requisite value—simply discard any lesser doom card as a minor action to keep your antagonists free and ready to act.

### Fight dirty, fight fair

Don’t pull punches, but fight fair. Don’t change your monsters’ stats halfway through a battle. If the players come up with an amazing plan and shove your big bad guy off of a tower, let it happen. Of course, what is good for the goose is good for the gander. If the players start shoving your Balrog off the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, you can pull their wizard off of it just as easily (if you have the appropriate card).

If the players are losing, they have a choice to make: stand and fight, run away, or talk their way out of it. The Disposition and Morale rules make sure that the players have ample opportunities to talk their way out of a fight at pretty much any stage.

## Creating interesting antagonists

Challenges that just consist of “smack the other guy dead” can be boring. Make fights with goals other than defeating the enemy. To this end, **create antagonists with potential energy**.

For example, if a Meatgrinder encounter simply lists “1–4 skeletons,” the Challenge Phase will consist of the antagonists whacking each other until one side expires from Wounds. However, if the Meatgrinder details, “1–4 skeletons in a **pit** that seem to be guarding a skeleton with a golden crown; the crowned skeleton is not moving,” the encounter gains more texture and raises several questions:

- Can the adventurers get the crown off of the king skeleton safely?

- What happens if they do? Does this trigger something?

- What is the story of these undead? How does it relate to this region of the Underworld?

- How can the pit be safely navigated by the adventurers?

As the guild moves through the Underworld, the potential energy from one antagonist will transfer onto another until eventually there is an explosion—with the players caught in the middle.

## Creating surprises

If things are beginning to get stagnant, introduce something that changes the Challenge dramatically. Let the potential energy transfer into **kinetic energy**.

Here’s a table to inspire ideas for different ways to shake things up. Glance at the top of the minor arcana discard pile for a random event:

# Running the Camp Phase

> [!lede]
> Camping scenes are moments of relative calm within the frenetic journey of the Underworld. They are explicitly times for a guild to regroup, role-play with each other, and recover near the warm light of the campfire before moving on into the waiting darkness; a time for the whole table to breathe a sigh of relief.

The GM’s job is two-fold during the Camp Phase:

- Act as the master of ceremonies to make sure that the Camp Phase flows smoothly, adjudicating any rules questions that come up

- Inject color into the scenes of the encampment

The latter is done by asking questions. For the vast majority of the game, you have to be “on”: you describe the setting, interpret the Meatgrinder results, role-play various characters. The Camp Phase is an opportunity for you to take a break, too. Give some of the narrative work to players: ask them to describe their gear, how they set up camp, what sort of rations they’re preparing, etc.

When the adventurers break bread (step 2), you can provide some discussion prompts and ask for a small scene to be role-played out. Frame these questions as subjects the entire guild is discussing: for example, “You’re all discussing the worst injuries you’ve had. What are your scars and where did you get them?”

Whenever the players riff on these questions, they provide you free ideas. Take notes about what the players are talking about at camp. Use them to seed the content of the City Phase!

- Did a player riff a story about their childhood sweetheart? Create a special City event where she invites them to her wedding. Will they accept?

- Did a player mention their mother’s famous lemon tart? When they get back to the City, she’s mailed them one—a free ration for the next crawl!

- Did a player mention an old enemy? Place their enemy in the City. Next time they surface, she approaches them on the street and asks to bury the hatchet. How will they react?

Don’t fall into the trap of thinking that punishment equals good role-playing. Don’t go and kill old loves, parents, friends. Rather, draw these personas into the story and create flash points for the players to act.

# Running the City Phase

> [!lede]
> Social encounters in the City aren’t like social encounters in the Underworld. In the Underworld, the characters are invaders. In the City, characters are part of the ecosystem. That is a fundamentally different paradigm.

> [!lede]
> Through role-playing, the City blooms for the players. However, the players must find the appropriate keys for the gates that are placed in front of them. The GM’s job is threefold: **Find what goals the players have, clearly define what is needed to achieve those goals, and create interesting obstacles.**

## Seed and grow goals

In the earliest sessions of the game, a player might have no specific goals or desires in the City. That’s fine. However, as a GM, it’s your job to lure them into the web of intrigue that makes the City Phase especially interesting. This is done by clearly showing the resources, boons, and wish fulfillment that can be gained by interacting with the City factions.

Initially, players may be interested in simple things: more money and more power. Again—that’s fine. Use these incentives as lures to a world of actual intrigue. As players get embroiled in factional strife, different goals will begin to emerge from base needs.

As with the Underworld, the City should be bizarre and complex. The City creation rules in Appendix D are designed to be evocative and inspiring. The setting is an anti-canon. Throw out ideas that don’t work for your game. Jam other shit in there. The people in the City should be evocative, scary, funny, weird, and interesting. The organizations should feel both rooted in history and fantastic. As a hint, nothing you can come up with will be weirder than history already is. Read a lot, collect a lot of ideas, and mash them together into your City.

Broadcast all the districts and special City Actions available to your players. **Create a shared document detailing all the things players can** do in **the City**. This lets players make informed decisions.

Players will be obliged to take studious notes, draw relationship maps, track favors owed, keep journals about the various likes and dislikes of various GM characters, etc. Figuring out how to navigate the social situations of the City Phase replicates the fun of discovery found during the Crawl Phase.

## Create a path to success

Don’t be coy about how players might achieve their goals. It’s not actually fun to be mysterious: it engenders a sense of mistrust between the GM and the players. If a player can declare a clear goal, you can provide a clear path towards that goal.

The [[09 - Chapter 9 - The City Phase#Support|Support]] City Action is **the main tool that players have to make meaningful progress towards open-ended goals**. Create a consistent metric for how you set complexity when adventurers are performing the Support action. Be open-minded when players propose actions that tick off complexity.

### Example

**Player:** I want to become the most respected mead maker in the City.

**GM:** OK. First, you have to actually build your meadery. That’s a Building action. “Meadery” is three syllables, so it’s going to take at least 150g to open your most basic, hole-in-the-wall joint. The fancier you want it to be, you can add more description for more money.

**Player:** I want it, literally, to be the “best meadery in the City.” That’s… eight syllables. So 400g.

**GM:** I agree. Then, you have to woo and court influential members of the aristocracy to hype your meadery. Starting complexity for basic tasks is at least complexity 3, but because being the best _anything_ in the City is difficult, let’s set the Support action to 5 complexity.

## Create obstacles

At the same time, you should make the paths to success fraught with peril. As the adventurers rise in the City, they’ll undoubtedly brush shoulders with powers that do not want any rivals. These rivals can **put** roadblocks in their way.

These dangers should not be easily perceived. Surprise setbacks are fair game; they test the players’ creativity and ability to react to new information.

Do not _unmake_ a player’s progress. If the GM burns down the meadery from the previous example, the player probably has good reason to be grumpy. Rather, introduce roadblocks.

For example, perhaps a rival bribes the tradesman’s union so that they will no longer do work for an adventurer—to use the Build action, the adventurer must get the union back on their side.

Importantly, the City is a place of laws. Murdering everything in the Underworld may be a strategy that gets results, but in the City it gets you jailed and executed. The players cannot kill their way out of social situations.

In general, we recommend not using the Challenge Phase rules at all in the City. If the players are contemplating some violence, reduce it to a Swords test. On a success, the players have won and can declare what sort of punishment they deal out: do they kill their opponent or simply slap them around a little? Depending on what damage is dealt and the relative social status of those involved, you can decide on the “appropriate” response from the All-Watch—the response from those in power is never commensurate.

## Completing quests and soft endings

The nature of the sandbox can make it tricky to know when the game is “over.” A more plot-driven game might be over when the players win or lose—the ring is cast into the fire, the Death Star is destroyed, everybody lives happily ever after. Not so in _His Majesty the Worm._ The players create their own quests and drive their own goals. When one adventurer’s quest is over, the rest of the guild still have quests to pursue.

An adventurer’s career ends in two ways: death or retirement. It should be the goal of every player to finish their adventurer’s quest and retire to safety in the City.

When one adventurer succeeds in their quest, it’s a good time to check in with your table.

- Do the players want to keep playing? Whose quest should they pursue next?

- Do the players want to continue playing _His Majesty the Worm_ but in a new setting? Should you all remake the City and Underworld (see Appendices D and E) and make a new guild?

- Does another player want to try their hand at GMing?

- Does the table want to play something else for a while? There’re tons of cool games out there.

You can keep playing _His Majesty the Worm_ or take a break. Just check in and make sure everybody is having as much fun as possible. The game might never be “over”—you can come back to the game again and again, creating a long and fondly-remembered history.

# Converting Other Games

An advantage of **OSR** games is that it’s simple to convert between them. _His Majesty the Worm_ plays like an oldschool game but is not a retroclone. A little braingrease must be applied to convert rules, modules, and monsters into this game format, although it’s more of an art than a skill. Here are some guidelines.

### Player character stats

Adventurers’ attribute ratings in _His Majesty the Worm_ are roughly equivalent to attribute bonuses in retroclone systems. For example, “Strength - 17 (+3)” would be “Swords 3.”

### OSR

| OSR | His Majesty the Worm |
|---|---|
| Strength bonus | Swords score |
| Dexterity bonus | Pentacles score |
| Wisdom bonus | Cups score |
| Charisma bonus | Wands score |
| Fighter | Path of Swords |
| Thief | Path of Pentacles |
| Cleric | Path of Cups |
| Magic-user | Path of Wands |
| Saving throws | Tests of fate |

### Monster stats

A retroclone monster’s **Hit Dice (HD) = their Health and Defense**. Monsters in _His Majesty the Worm_ are about as tough as in a retroclone.

Allocate the monster’s HD into a combination of Health or Defense. If a monster is very well armored (like a dragon), the Defense score will be higher. If a monster is very fast and hard to hit, the Health score will be higher. If you’re not sure, just make the monster’s Health and Defense roughly equivalent. For example, a 7HD dragon might have 2 Health, 5 Defense.

### Combat

“To-hit” and “damage” are mostly meaningless in _His Majesty the Worm._ A **Wound** is the average damage done by a single sword swing.

If a monster does an overwhelming amount of damage, you can represent that in different ways. A giant’s hammer might deal 2 Wounds, whereas a dragon’s unblockable fire breath might deal a Piercing Wound.

### Magic

When converting spells, first look for an equivalent spell in Appendix A. Feather Fall can be represented by the Feather spell, Cause Wounds can be represented by Withering, and so on.

Spells beyond the 2nd level might be beyond the scope of adventurer abilities in this game. If they are a core part of a monster’s move set, look at the [[01 - Chapter 1 - The Basics#Effects|Effects]] list and make a judgment about how best to represent that power here.

# Inspirational and Educational Reading

> [!lede]
> Here are some things that we think are awesome. _His Majesty the Worm_ wouldn’t exist without them. If you consume this media, you’ll get what we’re going for with this game.

## Core inspiration

The central influences on _His Majesty the Worm_ all happen to be comics. Not sure why comics were the main inspiration here; something about the visual medium and the vaguely alternate aesthetic? We don't know!

### Dungeon Meshi by Ryoko Kui

Dungeon Meshi is the most thoughtful treatment of the physicality of dungeoneering that we've ever read. The characters, their gear, and their journeys have a practical weight. Its considerations of exploration, food, and exhaustion are fun and evocative. This should be required reading for anybody that wants to ignore mundane gear or avoid tracking rations.

You can run _His Majesty the Worm_ as a _Dungeon Meshi_ RPG if you swap the word “alchemy” for “cooking.”

### House of Orr by Nolan T. Jones, Rilley Dutton, and Richard Zayas and artist Victoria Grace Elliott

A defunct and unfinished webcomic that is currently super hard to find on the internet. As such, apologies for including it here.

That said, _House of Orr_ genuinely shaped our thinking about “the party as a character.” In the setting, political capital is held by adventuring guilds. A struggling guild, the titular House of Orr (who, by tradition, requires all members to take on a new name with the element “Orr”—Orrlock, Orrdalf, Orrwyn, etc.), hopes to establish itself as a political power. With its whimsical elements, _House of Orr_ is genuinely delightful. It evokes the sense of playfulness that we want a game of _His Majesty the Worm_ to have.

### I Roved Out In Search of Truth and Love by Alexis Flowers

A “warmly pornographic” comic. If you strip out all the pornography, you have a super fun fantasy story. If you strip out all the fantasy story, you have some damn good smut. It is humorous and beautiful in a way that we want a game of _His Majesty the Worm_ to be.

By default, _His Majesty the Worm_ isn't pornographic—that would require a lot of buy-in, consent tools, etc. It's not the sort of game we’re trying to run 99% of the time. It is, however, an “adult” game. Adventurers can buy into relationships with each other by electing the Lovers Bond.

### Rat Queens by Kurtis J. Wiebe and Roc Upchurch

Goddamn, _Rat Queens_ rules. The friendship and interactions between the _Rat Queens_ illustrates the inter-party relationships of _His Majesty the Worm._ We want every adventure to delve into each character’s motivations, backstory, and friendships in the same way that _Rat Queens_ does.

The one-two punches (e.g., the Betty Climber) that the _Rat Queens_ perform in combat are half the reason the Challenge system works the way it does, too. Aid each other in combat to get the **BIG** numbers.

## Movies

### As Above, So Below

A found footage film where archaeologists-cum-urban-explorers delve into the Paris catacombs. They find Templar tombs, treasure, out-of-place artifacts, demons, and doom. It’s a great example of having a mythic underworld sitting under a major metropolitan area.

### Cube

A group of people wake up in a bizarre complex of interconnected cubes that are also death traps. Slowly, they start to piece together the puzzle of how to navigate them safely. A great example of how claustrophobic and terrifying crawling through a deathtrap dungeon would be.

### Indiana Jones

The **pulp** content of the Indiana Jones movies makes them genre staples in terms of dungeon-crawling. Think about the cool traps in _Raiders of the Lost Ark_ or the puzzles in _The Last Crusade._ It’s rare to see such careful attention to dungeon-crawling deliberately called out in popular media.

### Labyrinth

A young woman’s baby brother is trapped in the center of a sprawling maze by the Goblin King and she has until midnight to rescue him. As she adventures through the maze, she solves riddles, makes allies, and overcomes challenges. Dungeon-crawling at its most delightful.

### Spine of Night

Although it’s not about dungeon crawling, the sheer inventiveness of its ultra-gory, heavy-metal aesthetic is hard to rival. It deals with brutal warriors, greedy scholars, winged assassins, and power-mad sorcerers delving into powers that mortals were not meant to know. Watch it and be inspired.

## Books

### House of Leaves by Mark Z. Danielewski

Several narratives beautifully woven together: an academic critique of a found footage horror film, the personal journal of a man’s descent into schizophrenia, letters from a character’s mother. _House of Leaves_ plays with text in a way that few novels do. People say it’s weird but it’s honestly pretty gettable if you’re willing to give it a try.

### Invisible Cities by Italo Calvino

Framed as a conversation between Marco Polo and Kublai Khan, _Invisible Cities_ lists fifty-five fictitious cities and their fictitious histories. As a meditation on memory, it is excellent, compelling the reader to search for hidden meanings in the text. It is an example of what is meant by the term “**anti-canon**.”

### Piranesi by Susanna Clarke

The titular character lives (almost) alone in a museum world comprising three floors: a flooded basement, sprawling central corridors, and the occasional bird winging through the upper stories. An excellent example of what a mythic Underworld might feel like.

### Tombs of Atuan by Ursula K. LeGuin

In this novel, the main character is a priestess of the Old Powers. She tends a lightless labyrinth of tombs—a labyrinth with a dark, unknown purpose. Forty-percent of the story is told without the visual sense; this seems like an impossible task but is wildly successful.

Ultimately, this novel is one of the finest examples of fantasy fiction in the English language.

## Blogs

_His Majesty the Worm_ suckled on the terrible, sweet milk that came out of the blogosphere during the nascence of the OSR movement. All posts listed below are online as of this printing.

### “Blorb Principles,” from Idiomdrottning by Sandra Snan

“Blorb” is a funny name given to a prep style that emphasizes preparing the game space thoroughly before players begin to interact with it. It advocates creating a robust setting up front so that the GM has to do very little (if any) improv during the game.

In order of precedence, blorb prioritizes: 1. Prep 2. Procedure 3. Ideas that fill in the gaps between prep and procedure

If this appeals to you, you will find a game type analogous to how _His Majesty the Worm_ was played during its seven years of playtesting.

**Link:** https://idiomdrottning.org/blorb-principles

### “Dungeon Checklist,” from Goblinpunch by Arnold K

### “The Dungeon as Mythic Underworld,” from Philotomy’s Musings by Jason Cone

Philotomy’s Musings is a collection of interpretive essays about OD&D that contained a revolutionary way to re-imagine megadungeons. The way it frames a dungeon as an inherently mythic space served as an inspiration for the core setting of this game.

### “The ICI Doctrine: Information, Choice, Impact,” from Bastionland by Chris McDowall

This essay articulates that creating a true sense of player agency is done by emphasizing information, choice, and impact. This is a fundamental text to understand how to best organize the conversation of role-playing games from a GM’s perspective.
